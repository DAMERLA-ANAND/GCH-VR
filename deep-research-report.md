# 1. What’s broken today – Cardmembers vs. Merchants  

Cardholders report **slow, opaque dispute handling**.  Studies show 66% of fraud victims would *switch banks* if their dispute is handled slowly or poorly.  In interviews and forums, consumers complain of *weeks-long waits*, no updates, and confusing rules.  For example, one cardholder describes a months‑long battle (and even needing to threaten small claims court) just to recover a mistaken charge.  Many consumers don’t even know their rights: the CFPB found buyers are often *unaware of time limits* or that issuers “would not assist” with merchant refunds.  In practice, cardmembers feel forced into phone calls or paperwork to justify charges they never received, only to be handed a provisional credit and left in limbo.  This frustrates customers and erodes trust – one industry report notes customers *expect* disputes in real time, yet issuers still take ~2 weeks. 

*Image: A cardholder frustrated by a slow phone-based dispute process*.  Merchants, by contrast, complain that **chargebacks feel one-sided and unfair**.  Small businesses describe *“losing product, money, an extra fee, and [their] account [being] dinged”* whenever a buyer errors and simply files a chargeback.  Sellers say they’re often blind to the customer’s story – disputes arrive with little detail – and the bank “sides with” the cardholder by default.  They decry rampant “friendly fraud”: buyers abusing chargebacks to get free returns, with merchants left holding the cost.  In one merchant forum, owners note that even when customer admissions show the buyer was wrong, *merchants still lose* the dispute.  In short, merchants feel **powerless** and overburdened: disputes are costly (fees, lost goods, higher interchange) and their own evidence rarely prevails.  They cite “transparency […] sorely lacking” in today’s process.  In surveys, merchants rate chargeback handling as a top pain, noting that banks delegate disputes to call centers with an obvious conflict of interest favoring the cardholder.  

**Key takeaways:** the current process routinely takes weeks, lacks transparency, and forces one party’s evidence to dominate. Cardholders want *speed, clarity, and minimal effort*, while merchants demand *fairness, insight into buyer claims*, and reprieve from frivolous disputes.  These unmet needs – slow resolution for customers and biased, opaque adjudication for merchants – motivate our design.  

# 2. How disputes work today – and why that fails  

Today’s dispute flow is governed by card-network rules.  Typically, the cardholder “disputes” a posted charge via the issuer.  The issuer *provisionally credits* the account and contacts the acquirer (merchant’s bank) to initiate a chargeback.  The acquirer immediately debits the merchant’s account (and adds a chargeback fee).  The merchant is then notified of the reason code and given a brief window (often 7–21 days) to respond.  If the merchant sends evidence (“representment”), the issuer reviews and a final decision is made; if the merchant ignores it, the chargeback stands.  Amex, for example, enforces a 6–8 week investigation period before closing a case – after which, without a merchant response, the cardmember wins by default.  

**Networks and tools:** Each network (Visa, Mastercard, Amex, etc.) has its own codes and deadlines. Visa’s new “Claims Resolution” system categorizes disputes into Fraud, Authorization, Processing, or Customer-Dispute classes; Mastercard groups many customer disputes under one code.  In practice, disputes often start as “inquiries” (short-form queries) but quickly escalate.  For Visa, non-fraud inquiries enter a “collaboration” stage giving the merchant a short chance to resolve before a full chargeback; Mastercard usually skips straight to chargeback.  Either way, the merchant typically has only a few weeks to respond or forfeit.  Discover and other issuers likewise follow Reg Z rules (60-day dispute windows) but then defer to network protocols.  

A few partial improvements exist.  **Pre-dispute alerts** (e.g. Ethoca, Verifi) notify merchants *as soon as* a cardholder indicates a dispute, giving them a 48–72hr window to issue a refund and stop a chargeback.  Visa’s “Rapid Dispute Resolution” (RDR) lets merchants automatically pre-authorize refunds on certain small-claim disputes.  Some issuers provide apps to track dispute status.  However, these tools only prevent a subset of cases (mostly fraud or simple mistakes), and depend on merchant participation.  The core arbitration – evidence exchange via banks – still happens offline or via slow email/mail, taking days to weeks.  

**Why it falls short:**  This system fails its users for several reasons.  Technically, the workflow is manual and fragmented: no single platform connects cardholder, issuer, and merchant.  Evidence (receipts, comms) moves by email or fax, and each side often hears only its own narrative.  Procedurally, it’s a binary fight – the card network doesn’t mediate or allow negotiation.  Economically, issuers absorb dispute costs (around \$90 each) but have little reward for speeding things up, and merchants face escalating fees and fines (Visa/MC will even threaten to terminate merchants with high ratios).  Incentives are misaligned: issuers want to minimize losses, merchants want every chance to avoid refunds, and cardholders want quick relief.  This trifecta yields *long waits* (weeks before final call), *frustration* (missing clear timelines), and *default wins* for whichever side submits the required paperwork, not necessarily the rightful party.  

# 3. Proposed system design  

**Overview:** We propose a unified, online Dispute Resolution Platform that **integrates cardholders, issuers, and merchants in one workflow**, automating evidence collection and verdicts.  Every design choice ties back to a pain point we found: we build an **evidence-gathering portal, real-time interaction layer, and automated decision engine** so disputes resolve in minutes instead of weeks, transparently and fairly to both sides.  

- **Cardmember interface:** Disputes start in a mobile/web app.  Instead of a phone call, the cardmember files the dispute through a guided form.  The app *prompts the customer to explain why* (e.g. “merchant didn’t ship item,” “I was charged twice”), echoing recommendations that issuers capture dispute reasons upfront.  The user can upload receipts, photos, order confirmations or chat logs.  The app links each disputed charge to transaction data (time, amount, merchant name) already known by the issuer.  This solves the confusion noted by CFPB: cardholders often lack clarity on deadlines or how to prove a claim.  Our app immediately shows the 60-day limit and next steps, and even offers FAQs.  

- **Merchant interface:** Simultaneously, the platform alerts the merchant (via email or push to their own portal) that a dispute has been filed.  The merchant sees the cardmember’s claim and uploads any counter-evidence (e.g. proof of shipment, order notes).  We mirror solutions like Ethoca alerts but extend them to all disputes, not just fraud.  Crucially, this is *two-way in real time*: the merchant can ask the cardholder for clarification within the system, or even propose an immediate partial refund (like Visa RDR’s concept).  This addresses the merchants’ call for transparency – they aren’t “left in the dark” until chargeback notice arrives.  

- **Evidence parsing & rule engine:** Under the hood, an automated engine applies the card networks’ dispute rules to the submitted data.  For instance, if the customer claims “item never received” and the merchant’s tracking shows delivery to a wrong address provided by the customer, the rules might favor the merchant.  The system is essentially a *rules-based expert system* (using the networks’ reason-code logic) augmented by simple machine learning for edge cases (e.g. classifying “item not as described”).  This algorithmic core dramatically speeds up what today is a manual review.  Because the logic is transparent, its conclusions can be explained in plain language to both sides (e.g. “We found shipping proof vs. your claim, so the refund is denied”). 

- **Dispute tracking & fairness features:** The platform logs all interactions and outcomes.  Cardmembers can see “Your dispute is at Stage X – awaiting merchant response” in the app, reducing needless calls.  We also track each customer’s dispute history: if someone disputes charges serially, the system flags them (as recommended by industry experts) and may escalate or add checks to prevent abuse.  On the merchant side, analytics show chargeback trends so they can preemptively adjust (again, as [75] suggests).  To ensure neutrality, an independent audit trail is maintained, and both sides have an appeal window to request a final review (perhaps by a human under Reg Z rules).  

- **Data flows:** When a dispute is filed, the platform automatically pauses the charge (as issuers do) and reserves funds.  It then pushes all relevant data from issuer/merchant databases into the dispute portal.  The decision engine evaluates evidence in real time (targeting resolution *within minutes or hours*).  Funds are either released back to the customer or returned to the merchant in one step.  This contrasts with today’s back-and-forth: now we combine stage 1 (preliminary investigation) and stage 2 (representment) into a unified flow.  

- **Model choices & feasibility:** The tech stack is standard web architecture – secure web front-ends for cardmembers and merchants, a central database, and a rules engine (which could be built as a set of business rules or a simple AI classifier trained on past cases).  OCR and keyword extraction help parse receipts/emails.  All major banks already have similar capabilities (fraud systems, document processing).  This is well within reach of a small fintech or a bank’s IT team, especially since it layers on existing card transactions and user identity.  We’re **not inventing new tech**, just orchestrating known components more intelligently.  

- **Grounding in findings:** Each feature addresses a real pain point.  Consumers **get instant status and closure**, fixing the “slow, frustrating” process they warn will cost customer loyalty.  Merchants **gain visibility and voice**, correcting today’s “opaque, biased” experience.  Requiring both sides to engage (and see the same facts) promotes fairness: no more one-sided chargebacks.  For example, if the evidence clearly shows a merchant’s error, the system can auto-refund immediately (saving time) – but if not, it protects the merchant from an unjust loss.  By design, cardmembers can’t abuse the system undetected, and merchants can’t simply ignore legitimate claims.  

- **Trade-offs:** We **focus solely on dispute resolution**, not on fraud screening or new payment tech.  We assume the card networks will accept electronic submissions (a growing trend) and that small-dollar disputes will go through this channel first.  Complex legal claims (like multi-year warranties) might still require traditional escalation.  We do *not* require perfect data from consumers (they can’t always produce a PDF bill), but the system is robust to partial info.  Developing the expert-rule engine is substantial, but we plan a phased approach: start with the most common dispute categories (non-delivery, unauthorized charge) and expand.  We avoid unproven gimmicks (no blockchain or untrained AI); the core is deterministic and explainable.  

# 4. Benefits, validation, and fairness checks  

**Expected impact:** If built, this platform would **drastically cut dispute times**.  Today’s cycle (up to 6–8 weeks) would shrink to minutes or hours for routine cases.  We estimate a >90% reduction in resolution time, based on automating steps.  Customers see money (or know the outcome) almost immediately, improving satisfaction.  Preliminary pilots could measure this directly.  Merchants would see fewer chargebacks escalate (since minor issues would be settled instantly) and regain revenue faster when they win a dispute.  

We would track metrics such as “Time to resolution” and “Case error rate.”  For accuracy, we’d compare the platform’s decisions against historical outcomes (audit a sample of cases).  Early on, some disputes might still require manual review – but our goal is that >95% can be handled automatically with high confidence.  We will also monitor for unintended bias: for example, if the algorithm systematically favors cardholders, we’d recalibrate.  We can measure “win rates” by side; an unbiased system should not skew heavily one way given equal evidence.  

**Business value:** For an issuer or network, this delivers clear ROI.  Faster dispute handling reduces call-center volume (customers get answers in-app, as [75] suggests).  Satisfied cardmembers stay loyal: recall 66% would switch after a bad dispute experience, so improving speed and clarity protects the bank’s customer base.  It also cuts fraud/friendly-fraud losses by flagging serial abusers.  Cost savings are real: each dispute costs ~$90 to process, so even modest automation pays off.  On the merchant side, fewer unfounded chargebacks means lower interchange and better retailer relations.  Finally, regulators reward smooth processes: under Reg E and Z banks must investigate disputes promptly; our system would ensure compliance and reduce CFPB complaints (a known issue).  

**Ensuring fairness:** We would subject the platform to review by impartial auditors (internal compliance or even the card network’s compliance team) to ensure neither side is systematically disadvantaged.  If a customer loses, the system will explain exactly why (e.g. “our records show delivery confirmed”) – avoiding the “black box” feel of today’s process.  Cardmembers and merchants both see the evidence used.  In edge cases, they can still escalate to a manual appeal.  By avoiding human reviewers, we *reduce* the human bias reported in call centers, and by using consistent rules we apply the same standard to everyone.  

**Conclusion:** In sum, our design directly addresses the discovered pain points: it **speeds up** resolution (satisfying customers’ demand for real-time answers), **improves transparency** (giving merchants and customers the same information), and **balances fairness** (both sides contribute evidence, and repeat disputers are tracked).  If successful, we expect dispute times to fall from weeks to minutes, while keeping customer and merchant satisfaction high.  This would retain cardmembers (protecting revenue) and keep merchants happy (protecting acceptance rates), making it worth the issuer/network’s investment.  Every major design choice here is grounded in the complaints and gaps we found – it’s not just a wishlist, but a coordinated solution to fix the broken parts of today’s dispute process.
